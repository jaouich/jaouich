[Slide 1 · 14s]
Welcome to Jerry Documentaries. In this first episode we travel to the largest hot desert on Earth, the Sahara, and we discover that it is very different from the pictures in our minds.

[Slide 2 · 15.6s]
The Sahara covers about nine million square kilometers, an area close to the size of the United States. It stretches across North Africa, from the Atlantic Ocean in the west to the Red Sea in the east.

[Slide 3 · 14.4s]
Many people imagine endless sand, but sand dunes cover only a part of the Sahara. Most of it is bare rock plateaus and gravel plains, and there are even high mountains inside the desert.

[Slide 4 · 14.8s]
The weather is extreme. Days can be extremely hot, and because the air is dry, nights can be cold. Rain is rare in many areas, and some places can go years without a single storm.

[Slide 5 · 14.4s]
But the Sahara was not always a desert. Several thousand years ago, a wetter period turned large parts of it into grasslands with lakes, rivers and animals. Scientists call it the African Humid Period.

[Slide 6 · 15.2s]
We know this from rock art. In places like Tassili n'Ajjer in Algeria, ancient people painted cattle, giraffes and hunters on the stone. These images are thousands of years old and show a very different landscape.

[Slide 7 · 14.8s]
Later, the desert became a road for trade. Caravans of camels carried salt, gold and other goods between North Africa and West Africa, and trading cities such as Timbuktu became centers of wealth and learning.

[Slide 8 · 14.8s]
Life in the Sahara gathers around water. In oases, underground water feeds date palms and gardens. Communities such as the Amazigh and the Tuareg have lived here for centuries, with deep knowledge of the desert.

[Slide 9 · 16.4s]
Even the Sahara's dust has a story. Winds lift fine dust and carry it across the Atlantic Ocean, where it can reach the Amazon rainforest and bring minerals to its soil. Our planet is more connected than we think.
